A clear quote prevents most money disputes. It shows the client what they are paying for, what is not included, and how changes are handled. This guide gives a structure you can adapt. The amounts below are placeholders; set your own from your costs and local market. This is general advice, not legal or tax advice, so check requirements for contracts and VAT where you work.

## Quote, estimate or budget?

- **Design fee proposal:** what you charge for your services.
- **Budget:** the client's total spend on products, trades and fees, often prepared in stages.
- **Quote:** a fixed price for defined work.
- **Estimate:** a best guess that can move; label it clearly.

Be explicit about which one you are issuing. Mixing them is a common source of misunderstanding.

## Decide how you charge

Common models are a fixed fee per phase or project, an hourly rate, a percentage of project cost, or a mark-up on products. Each has trade-offs: fixed fees need a tight scope, hourly rates need good time records, percentages need an agreed cost base. Whichever you use, write down how it is calculated and when it is invoiced.

## Build the quote in steps

1. **Define scope.** List rooms, services and deliverables. Name what is excluded.
2. **Split into phases.** Match your [workflow stages](/guides/interior-design-project-workflow-from-brief-to-handover/), so fees can be tied to approvals.
3. **List line items.** Group by category: design fee, furniture, lighting, finishes, soft furnishings, trades, delivery, contingency.
4. **Add allowances where choices are not made.** An allowance is a placeholder amount for an item not yet selected; mark it as such.
5. **State assumptions and exclusions.** For example: access, structural work, permits, third-party delays.
6. **Set payment terms.** Deposit, stage payments, due dates, and what happens on late payment.
7. **Add validity and change terms.** How long prices hold, and how variations are priced.

## Contingency and allowances

Hidden conditions and late decisions are normal. A contingency line gives you room to absorb them without renegotiating. The percentage is your judgement and depends on project type and how much is already specified; state what it covers and what happens to unused amounts. Track allowances separately so the client sees when a selection exceeds the placeholder.

## Present the budget so it can be approved

- Show totals by category first, detail underneath.
- Mark what is fixed, what is estimated and what is an allowance.
- Offer a priority list: what to cut or defer if the total is too high.
- Get written acceptance before ordering.

## Common mistakes

- Vague scope, with no exclusions.
- Quoting products without checking current prices and lead times.
- No allowance labelling, so a changed selection looks like an overrun.
- No contingency.
- Starting work without a signed quote and deposit.
- Not recording variations as they happen.

## Checklist before sending

- Scope and exclusions written.
- Every line has a reference, quantity and unit.
- Allowances and estimates labelled.
- Tax treatment stated.
- Payment terms and validity date included.
- Variation process explained.
- Placeholders replaced with checked figures.
