Every floor plan, furniture layout and flooring order depends on the measurements you take on site. Software cannot fix a wrong number. This guide covers a simple, repeatable method and when to use a tape or a laser.

## Tape or laser?

A **tape measure** is cheap, needs no batteries and works everywhere. Its weak points are length and sag: measuring a long room alone is awkward, and you need a second person to hold the end.

A **laser distance measurer** lets one person measure a whole room by pointing at a wall. Models also calculate area, volume and indirect height. For example, Bosch lists the GLM 50-27 CG with a 0.05 to 50 m range and typical accuracy of ±1.5 mm, and Bluetooth for its app; the Hilti PD-I lists Bluetooth connection with partner apps including magicplan. Lasers can struggle on glass, mirrors or in very bright light, so keep a tape in the bag as a backup. Our [laser measurer picks](/gear/best-laser-distance-measurers/) are based on published specs, not hands-on testing.

A sensible kit is a laser for lengths, a tape for small items and tight spots, a notebook or tablet, a pencil and a camera.

## Step by step

1. **Sketch the room by hand first.** Draw a rough outline, not to scale, with each wall labelled. Mark doors, windows, alcoves and chimney breasts.
2. **Measure wall by wall.** Go around in one direction, measuring each wall at about waist height, and write each number directly on the sketch.
3. **Measure both directions.** Take length and width, then measure again along each wall at a different point. Old walls are often not square, so two readings of the same wall may differ.
4. **Take a diagonal.** Measuring corner to corner helps you check that the room is square. If the diagonals differ, record both.
5. **Measure openings.** Record width and height of each door and window, the distance from the corner to each opening, and the sill height.
6. **Measure ceiling height.** Check it in more than one place, and note beams, bulkheads and sloping ceilings.
7. **Note fixed items.** Radiators, sockets, switches, pipes and built-in units all affect furniture placement.
8. **Photograph everything.** Take wide photos of each wall and close-ups of anything unusual.
9. **Check the total.** The segments along each wall should add up to the full wall length. If they do not, re-measure before you leave.

## Habits that prevent errors

- Use one unit throughout and write it on the sketch.
- Measure from the same height each time.
- Measure the wall surface, not skirting boards, unless you note otherwise.
- Write down numbers immediately and read them back.
- Never round on site. Round later in the software if needed.
- Ask whether a figure is finished or unfinished wall if renovation work is planned.

## Getting measurements into software

Some tools accept readings from a phone. [magicplan](/reviews/magicplan-review/) creates floor plans on a phone or tablet with manual and laser-assisted measurements, and LiDAR scanning on iOS Pro devices, although its marketing is aimed at restoration teams. Otherwise, draw the room from your sketch in a planner such as [RoomSketcher](/reviews/roomsketcher-review/), [Planner 5D](/reviews/planner-5d-review/) or [Sweet Home 3D](/reviews/sweet-home-3d-review/). For marking up an existing drawing at scale on an iPad, [Morpholio Trace](/reviews/morpholio-trace-review/) lists scale tools and drawing over PDFs and photos. After entering the room, compare diagonals and a few distances in the software with your notes.

## Checklist

- [ ] Rough sketch drawn with all walls labelled
- [ ] Each wall measured, ideally twice
- [ ] Diagonals recorded
- [ ] Doors and windows: width, height, position, sill height
- [ ] Ceiling height in several places
- [ ] Radiators, sockets and fixed items marked
- [ ] Photos of every wall
- [ ] Segment totals checked against full wall lengths
- [ ] Unit written on the sketch

Ten extra minutes on site usually saves a second visit, and a plan built from careful numbers is the base for everything that follows, from furniture layouts to rendering.
